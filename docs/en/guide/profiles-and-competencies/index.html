Team profiles, job descriptions and competency levels

Is the right person in the right role? Three hiring criteria, role-based profiles, sample job descriptions, four competency levels and a 90-day model.

5 min readUpdated: 2026-11-28

Three hiring criteria

Knowledge

Industry, products, customer types and basic commercial maths (margin, cost, payment terms).

Skills

Questioning, listening, persuasion, objection handling, planning, numerical analysis.

Behaviour

Customer focus, resilience, discipline, integrity, teamwork.

Knowledge is the fastest to teach and behaviour the hardest to change. So prioritise behaviour when hiring and knowledge and skills when training.

Role-based profiles

Role Key traits Profile to avoid
New Business Specialist (hunter) High energy, resilience to rejection, quick rapport, competitiveness Seeks routine and familiar customers
Account Manager (farmer) Analytical, patient, consultative, consistent follow-up Focused only on closing, weak follow-up
Key Account Manager Strategic thinking, negotiation, project management, senior communication Busy with individual orders
Sales Force Manager Coaching, role modelling, planning, fairness The “best seller” who can’t manage
Regional Manager Developing managers, analysis, resource allocation Only asks for reports
Head of Sales Force Strategy, financial literacy, change management, cross-functional influence Detached from the field

Why job descriptions matter

A job description clarifies expectations, underpins hiring and appraisal, and prevents gaps and overlaps between roles. It should include: purpose · main responsibilities · KPIs · decision authority · core competencies · qualifications · reporting line.

Example: Account Manager

Purpose: Grow the business the assigned portfolio customers do with the company, profitably and sustainably.

Main responsibilities:

  • Visit the portfolio at the frequency set by the segment recipe
  • Prepare and execute an annual growth plan for every A and B customer
  • Increase category penetration and share of wallet
  • Keep customer data (potential, decision-maker, competitor) up to date
  • Follow up collections and prevent overdue receivables
  • Resolve customer issues with the relevant departments and report back

KPIs: sales and gross profit · active customers · penetration · share of wallet · collections · coaching score.

Example: Sales Force Manager

Purpose: Ensure the team reaches its targets by developing reps’ competencies in the field.

Main responsibilities: target breakdown and portfolio balance · weekly dashboard and monthly one-to-ones · at least one full field-coaching day per rep per month · identifying training needs · taking part in hiring and onboarding · coordinating with other departments.

Key success activities

Whatever the role, successful salespeople repeat the same activities: planned visits · preparation · objective-led conversations · notes and records · following up promises · analysing their own results · updating product and market knowledge. The manager’s job is to turn these into habits.

Four competency levels

Level Definition Needs Management style
Beginner New to the job, highly motivated, limited knowledge Information, clear instructions, frequent feedback Directing
Developing Does the basics but inconsistently; motivation may dip Context, cause and effect, encouragement Discussing / explaining
Competent Does what is expected independently Room, responsibility, freedom to make mistakes Supporting
Expert A role model who can teach others Trust, challenge, authority Delegating
Levels are not job titles

The same person can be an expert in product knowledge and developing in negotiation. Assess by competency; base pay and career decisions on the overall level. Validate your level definitions with HR before use.

The interview process

  1. Screening: CV and a short phone call; basic fit (area, licence, expectations).
  2. Competency interview: questions about past behaviour. “Tell me about a time you won a customer from a competitor: what did you do, and what happened?”
  3. Role play: a short sales conversation scenario; observe questioning and objection handling.
  4. Field day (final candidates): half a day with an experienced rep; the candidate sees the job and the company sees the candidate.
  5. Reference check and decision.

Common assessment mistakes

Looking only at industry experience · mistaking talkativeness for selling skill · assuming big-company experience means quality · skipping references · ignoring role fit · not probing profit awareness · ignoring coaching ability in manager candidates · not asking for concrete examples.

The first 90 days

Period Focus Assessment
Days 1–30 Company, products, systems, customer types; joint visits with an experienced rep Knowledge check, observation notes
Days 31–60 Own visits accompanied by the manager; a small portfolio Coaching form, visit quality
Days 61–90 Full portfolio, first targets Results + behaviour; confirmation decision

Run a review halfway through probation too: the new hire learns their strengths and weaknesses and gets a chance to improve before the final decision.

Manager check questions

  • Do we have a written, current job description for every role?
  • Do our interviews ask for concrete examples of past behaviour?
  • Are new hires’ first 90 days planned, or is it “go out and learn”?
  • Do I know each person’s competency level and adapt my style?

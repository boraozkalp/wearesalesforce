The customer visit and the sales conversation

An eight-step visit structure from preparation to analysis; questioning, listening, benefit-led persuasion, objection handling, closing and negotiation.

The purpose of a visit

Every visit has one question: What will be different for the customer after this visit? A new category trial, a larger order, a resolved complaint, an introduction to a decision-maker… Without an answer, the visit is small talk — the most expensive form of one-to-one selling.

The eight-step visit

1. Preparation

An unprepared visit spends the customer’s time on your learning curve. Prepare four things:

  • Customer record: recent purchases, frequency, categories not bought, open complaints, promises from the last visit.
  • Objective: one main objective in one sentence plus a fallback. “Get a trial order for breakfast products; if not, book a tasting with the chef.”
  • Arguments: 2–3 benefit statements tailored to the customer type and motivation.
  • Tools: offer, samples, catalogue, price list, collection information.

2. Opening: first impressions

You don’t get a second chance at a first impression.

  • Personal space: respect it; let the customer set the distance.
  • First sentences: short, warm and specific. Open with an observation from your preparation (“I saw the new breakfast plate on your menu last week…”).
  • Agenda: state the purpose and length of the visit in the first minute and get agreement.

Communication is more than words

Research suggests that, especially when feelings and attitudes are conveyed, tone of voice and body language carry more weight than the words themselves. Eye contact, posture, tone and pace either support or undermine what you say.

Messages get lost on the way

Between what you want to say and what the customer remembers and can pass on, something is lost at every step: what I mean → what I say → what is heard → what is understood → what is accepted → what is remembered → what can be passed on. Important messages should therefore be short, repeated and written down — especially when the person you meet must convince someone else without you.

3. Discovery: questioning and listening

Good salespeople talk less and ask more. The customer should talk for at least half the conversation.

Question type Purpose Example
Open Get information and views “How do you plan your breakfast service?”
Closed Confirm, clarify “Would two deliveries a week be enough?”
Reflective Check understanding “So the real issue is running out at weekends, right?”
Impact Show the size of the need “How does running out affect service?”

Funnel technique: start with broad open questions, narrow down based on answers, confirm with closed questions.

Active listening

  • Take notes and mark the customer’s key words (“cost”, “speed”, “reliability”). You will use the same words when persuading.
  • Don’t interrupt; don’t fear silence.
  • Summarise what you heard in your own words.
  • Listen to what is not said: hesitation, change of tone, avoided topics.

Buying motivations

Motivation What the customer seeks Useful words
Gain Lower cost, margin, yield savings, profit, yield, waste
Security Avoiding risk, guarantees reliable, standard, certified, guaranteed
Convenience Saving time and effort practical, ready, fast, one-stop
Novelty Differentiation, trends new, first, trending, exclusive
Prestige Image, perceived quality premium, well-known, select
Relationship Trust, personal bond together, support, partnership

4. Persuasion: benefit-led presentation

Customers buy what a product does for them, not its features. Build every proposal with this chain:

  1. Situation: “You said you run out at weekends.”
  2. Idea: “We could add a Friday morning delivery.”
  3. How it works: “Order by Thursday evening and it’s with you by 10:00 on Friday.”
  4. Benefit: “So nothing drops off the Saturday breakfast menu, and you don’t hold extra stock.”
  5. Check: “Would that work for you?”

5. Handling objections

An objection is not a rejection; it is a sign of interest: the customer is thinking and voicing missing information or a concern. Answering an objection immediately with a discount is the most expensive mistake.

  1. Listen to the end without interrupting.
  2. Acknowledge: “I understand that concern.” (You accept that you heard it, not that they are right.)
  3. Clarify: “By price, do you mean the unit price or the total cost?”
  4. Respond with a benefit, proof, comparison or alternative.
  5. Confirm: “Does that answer your question?”
Type Signal Approach
Genuine objection Concrete and consistent Answer directly with evidence
Excuse Vague, keeps changing Ask “Apart from that, is there anything else stopping you?”
Prejudice Past experience or hearsay Offer a trial, references, a guarantee
Missing information Wrong assumption Politely give the correct information

6. Closing

Closing is not the end of the conversation; it is securing a commitment. When buying signals appear (questions about price, delivery, quantity; positive body language), move to close.

  • Direct: “Shall we set up the first order for Friday delivery?”
  • Alternative choice: “For the trial, would 5 kg or 10 kg suit you better?”
  • Summary: “We agree on quality and delivery; only the quantity is left.”
  • Next step: even without an order, a dated next step (“Let’s do a tasting with the chef on Tuesday at 11:00.”).

7. Recording

Log the visit note the same day: what was discussed, what was promised, what you learned and the objective of the next visit. Put promises in the calendar with dates and inform the relevant teams.

8. Review

A two-minute self-review: Did I reach my objective? What would I do differently? It’s the cheapest way to improve, even without a coach.

Customer communication styles

Style Signs Approach
Analytical Detail, data, slow decisions Give figures, evidence and written information; don’t rush
Driver Fast, direct, impatient Keep it short; lead with the result and benefit
Amiable Values relationships and trust, risk-averse Be calm; give reassurance and references
Expressive Energetic, social, big picture Talk vision and novelty; give details in writing later

Negotiation: the win-win principle

  • Know your alternative: decide beforehand what you will do if there is no deal; the side with the stronger alternative negotiates more comfortably.
  • Start high but defensible: the first offer anchors the negotiation.
  • Never concede for free: “If you … then we can …”
  • Talk interests, not positions: “Why is this price important?” opens alternative solutions.
  • Summarise the agreement in writing.

The 10 most common mistakes

  1. Going unprepared. 2. Visiting without an objective. 3. Talking more than listening. 4. Jumping to price. 5. Criticising competitors. 6. Not taking notes. 7. Answering objections with discounts. 8. Not closing. 9. Leaving the next step vague. 10. Not following up on promises.

Manager check questions

  • Does my team go into every visit with a written objective?
  • In conversations, does the customer or the rep talk more?
  • Is the first response to an objection a discount?
  • Are visit notes entered into the system the same day?

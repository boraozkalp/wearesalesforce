Training, coaching and competency development

Training is a system, not an event. Onboarding, basic and advanced training, measuring impact; the field coaching day, feedback rules and coaching models.

Training is more than passing on information

Training succeeds in the field, not in the classroom. Even when participants learn something new, they slip back into old habits within weeks if their manager doesn’t follow up in the field. An effective training system has four parts: needs analysis → training → field application and coaching → measurement.

70 / 20 / 10

A widely used approach holds that roughly 70% of adult learning happens through on-the-job experience, 20% through interaction with others (coaching, mentoring, feedback) and 10% through formal training. The ratios are not exact; the message is clear: classroom training doesn’t deliver without field coaching.

Main elements of a sales training system

Element For Content
Onboarding All new hires Company, customer types, range, systems, field observation
Product and category Reps Product knowledge, uses, customer benefit, competitor comparison
Sales process Reps 8-step visit, questioning, benefit-led persuasion, objections, closing
Negotiation Experienced reps, key accounts Preparation, alternatives, managing concessions, win-win
Data and systems Everyone CRM use, data quality, reading reports
Profit and collections Reps Margin calculation, impact of discounts, payment terms and risk
Management training Field and regional managers Coaching, performance conversations, target breakdown, hiring

Basic and advanced training

Basic training

For everyone starting a new role; teaches the key points of the job. Roughly one third theory (classroom or online), two thirds practice in the field with an experienced guide.

Advanced training

For people with a certain level of experience and potential; sharing best practice and specialist topics (negotiation, analysis, team management). Attendance on the manager’s recommendation.

Sample onboarding programme

Duration Topic
1 day Company, values, organisation, admin
3 days Store / warehouse / operations processes
5 days Customers, market and competitors; visits with an experienced rep
2–3 weeks Products, categories, solutions
2 weeks The sales role: tasks, systems, rules; first visits with the manager

A short knowledge check at the end (e.g. 80% correct threshold) and the manager’s observation notes feed into the probation decision.

Measuring training impact

A widely used four-level evaluation approach:

  1. Reaction: did participants find it useful? (survey)
  2. Learning: did knowledge and skills increase? (pre/post test, role play)
  3. Behaviour: do they act differently in the field? (coaching form, 30–90 days later)
  4. Results: did business indicators change? (penetration, average order, conversion rate)

Every participant writes a personal action plan at the end of the training, and their manager follows it up. Training that isn’t followed up is a cost.

Coaching: manager and coach

Manager hat Coach hat
Focuses on performance Focuses on competence
Measures results Observes behaviour
Runs the team, allocates resources Develops the individual
Asks “What happened?” Asks “How did you do it, and how could it be better?”

To be accepted as a coach, you first need a trustworthy management style: reliability, availability, supportive behaviour, role modelling and a positive attitude.

Performance = competence × motivation

Individual performance depends on competence (knowledge and skills) and motivation (confidence and willingness). Coaching aims to build competence while protecting motivation, and the style adapts to the person’s level (see Module 8): directing at first, explaining while developing, supporting when competent, delegating when expert.

A coaching day in the field

  1. Prepare (the day before): the person’s profile, targets, last coaching note and action plan; a route of at least 6 visits; the day’s coaching focus.
  2. Start the day: a warm opening; clarify the purpose and roles (“I’m observing; you lead with the customer.”); ask about expectations and how they are today.
  3. Observe the first visit without intervening, taking notes. Measure the gaps: knowledge, skill or behaviour?
  4. First feedback: their self-assessment first; then one strength and one development point.
  5. Agree the development goal for the day: no more than two focus points.
  6. Next visits: short feedback after each; if needed, role-model one visit and then have them repeat it.
  7. Lunch: a fuller review of the morning.
  8. Afternoon visits: the same loop; watch for progress.
  9. End-of-day review: go through the observation form together and agree a written action plan.
  10. Follow-up: promises made to customers, execution of the action plan, and a check at the next coaching day.
Tool

Record observations in a standard way with the Coaching observation form: 8 steps, 30 criteria, automatic percentages.

The coaching loop: Measure – Talk – Agree – Apply – Track – Recognise

  1. Measure the gap between expected and observed performance.
  2. Talk: ask for their view and listen actively.
  3. Agree the development goal and steps together.
  4. Apply: encourage them to try the new behaviour; demonstrate if needed.
  5. Track progress on the agreed date.
  6. Recognise progress concretely.

Feedback rules

  • Let the person assess themselves first.
  • Start with the positive and be specific (“Your second question uncovered the real problem.”).
  • Talk about behaviour, not personality.
  • Base it on observation, not assumption.
  • Limit development points to two.
  • Give it promptly: right after the visit, not in front of the customer.
  • Ask questions so they find the solution.
  • Agree the next step in writing.

Coaching conversations with GROW

Stage Example questions
Goal “What do you want to be different after this conversation?”
Reality “What is happening now? What have you tried, and with what result?”
Options “What else could you do? What would you do with no constraints?”
Will “Which one will you do, and when? How will we know it worked?”

Core competencies of professional coaching

International coaching standards look for these competencies in a professional coach (summarised): ethical practice · an open, curious and flexible coaching mindset · clear agreements in the coaching relationship · creating trust and safety · being fully present · active listening · evoking awareness through powerful questions, silence and metaphor · turning learning into action and supporting the person’s autonomy. A field manager doesn’t need to become a professional coach, but should bring these principles into everyday coaching.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating training as a one-off event. 2. No follow-up after training. 3. The same training for everyone. 4. Presenting products as features. 5. No role play. 6. Not training managers. 7. Not measuring results. 8. Turning coaching days into inspection days.

Manager check questions

  • Do I spend at least one full coaching day in the field with each rep every month?
  • Are my coaching notes and action plans written down and followed up?
  • Do we measure training impact at the behaviour and results levels?
  • Does my feedback focus on behaviour or on personality?

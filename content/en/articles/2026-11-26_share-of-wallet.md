A restaurant buys 120,000 a month from you. It shows up as a “good customer” in the sales report; the rep is happy, the manager is happy. But what if the restaurant’s total purchases from all suppliers are 1 million a month?

Then the picture changes: **your share of wallet is 12%**. 88% of the customer’s spend goes to competitors, and you will never see it by looking at sales.

## Sales show the past; share of wallet shows the future

| | Customer A | Customer B |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly sales (from us) | 120,000 | 120,000 |
| Total purchases (all suppliers) | 150,000 | 1,000,000 |
| Share of wallet | 80% | 12% |
| Right strategy | Protect, serve efficiently | Win share category by category |

In the sales report they look the same. In portfolio strategy they are opposites.

## How do I estimate potential?

You don’t need an exact figure; a reasonable estimate is enough. Common indicators in foodservice:

- **Restaurant:** seats × daily turns × average bill × food cost ratio
- **Hotel:** rooms × occupancy × food and beverage spend per guest
- **Café:** daily receipts × average receipt × cost ratio

Update the estimate regularly through conversations with the customer (what they buy from whom, and how often).

## What changes in the field?

1. **The visit objective changes:** from “take an order” to “become the second supplier for dairy”.
2. **The conversation changes:** you discuss the customer’s total buying structure, not price.
3. **The reward changes:** share or penetration is added to the bonus, steering reps towards growth.

> A customer with high sales but low share is the biggest opportunity in your portfolio. A customer with low sales but high share is your biggest risk: no room to grow, and if you lose them, you lose everything.
